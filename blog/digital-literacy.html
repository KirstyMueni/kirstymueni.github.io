Digital Literacy in Kenyan Schools: Skills for Learning and Life

By Kirsty Mueni · 6 min read

Digital literacy is more than knowing how to switch on a device. It is the ability to use technology confidently, safely and thoughtfully to learn, communicate, solve problems and create.

Why digital literacy matters

In a connected world, learners need to search for information, judge whether it is trustworthy, communicate respectfully and protect their personal information. These skills support learning across subjects and help young people participate in work and community life. Digital literacy also includes creativity: learners can use technology to write, code, design, record and share ideas.

From devices to meaningful learning

Providing devices is only one part of digital learning. Schools also need relevant learning materials, reliable technical support and teachers who feel prepared to integrate technology into lessons. UNESCO's account of Kenya's Digital Literacy Programme describes teacher preparation focused on planning ICT-supported lessons, creating learning activities and managing digital resources with learners. This reinforces an important principle: technology works best when it supports a clear learning goal, rather than becoming a goal on its own. Read UNESCO's overview of teacher preparation in Kenya.

Equity, access and local context

Schools do not all have the same access to connectivity, electricity, devices or technical support. A practical digital-literacy programme should account for those differences. Offline resources, shared-device activities and lessons that can continue when the internet is unavailable can help make learning more resilient. Materials should also be relevant to learners' languages, communities and everyday experiences.

Teach learners to be safe and discerning

Students should practise checking who created a source, comparing claims with other reliable information and recognising suspicious links or requests for personal details. They also need guidance on respectful communication, cyberbullying, privacy and asking a trusted adult for help. These habits are part of digital competence, not optional extras.

A practical way forward

Start with one learning need, choose an accessible tool or resource, and give learners time to practise. Offer teachers ongoing support and opportunities to share lesson ideas. Ask learners to demonstrate what they can do, such as finding and evaluating information or creating a short digital project. Review what worked, what barriers appeared and what should change next time.

Kenya's digital-literacy journey is strongest when infrastructure, teacher development and learner-centred teaching move together. The aim is not simply to put screens in classrooms. It is to help every learner gain the confidence and judgement to use technology for meaningful learning.
